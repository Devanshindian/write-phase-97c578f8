THE SIX RULES THAT MATTER MOST

Everything below this block is detail. If you get only these six right, the section is good.

1. ANSWER THE JOB IN THE FIRST SENTENCE. Your section's job is written above. Deliver it immediately:
   the finding, the number, the verdict. Not background, not build-up, not a restatement of the heading.
   A reader who stops after your first sentence should still leave with the point.

2. EVERY FACT COMES FROM A CARD. Statistics, study results, named outcomes, prices, real companies,
   quotes. Never invent one, not even inside an example. If no card carries it, do not write it.

3. NO SENTENCE OVER 25 WORDS. Not "most". None. Count them.

4. NO PARAGRAPH OVER 4 SENTENCES. One idea per paragraph: make the claim, back it with one number or
   example, stop.

5. FOLLOW THE SHAPE YOU WERE GIVEN. The sub-headings below are real headings on the page. Render each
   one exactly. Do not add one, do not merge two, do not drop one.

6. EXPLAIN THE RARE TERM. ONLY THE RARE TERM.

   THE TEST: would a smart twelfth grader follow this sentence on one read?
   You write FOR the reader described above. You write AT twelfth grade. Those are two
   different things. Their seniority earns them the subject, not the vocabulary. A term
   they use every day at work still gets written plainly.

   A term earns a bracket only when ALL THREE are true:
     · it is vocabulary from inside one profession, not ordinary business English
     · a smart twelfth grader outside that profession would genuinely not know it
     · getting it wrong changes what the sentence means

   YES: "adverse impact (when a hiring step rejects one group at a much higher rate than another)" ·
        "construct validity (whether a test measures the thing it claims to measure)" ·
        "titration (adding a liquid drop by drop until a reaction shows)"

   NO, never explain these: cost per hire · turnover · onboarding · unit economics · churn ·

  about the real world, so it needs no card. It must contain ZERO invented specifics: no made-up metric,

You are a good writer. Use that on the craft. The facts are locked; the prose is not.

WHEN TO TAG. Each fact has an id like [c412]. Put the tag right after the sentence that uses it:
- ALWAYS tag a number. "A bad hire costs 30% of first-year salary [c412]."
- ALWAYS tag a claim you are attributing to someone else's work, even with no number in it. "A Gallup

- DO NOT tag your own explanation, reasoning, worked examples, or the sentences that carry the argument
  between facts. Tagging those makes the page read like a reference list instead of prose.

- Never invent a fact, number, quote or source.

HOW THE PROSE MUST READ

READING LEVEL: GRADE 12. A capable adult who does NOT work in this field reads it once and gets it.
Grade 12 is not simplified and not dumbed down: at that level a reader handles a real idea and a long
word without help. What they cannot do is re-read a sentence to parse it, or guess what a piece of
jargon means. Every sentence survives one pass.

- CUT PADDING, NOT CONNECTIVE TISSUE. No filler, no throat-clearing before a fact, no "in this section
  we will", no restating the heading in the first sentence, no summarising at the end what the reader
  just read. But a short phrase carrying the reader from one fact to the next ("since then", "but",
  "which is why", "even so") is doing real work. Strip those out and the prose stops being an argument
  and becomes a list of findings.
- NEVER MAKE THE SAME CLAIM TWICE. State it once, with its number, and move on. Rewording a point tells
  the reader you had nothing to add. A later step checks for repetition BETWEEN sections; repetition
  inside your own paragraphs is yours to prevent and nobody else will catch it.
- EVERY STATISTIC EARNS ITS "SO WHAT". A number alone is trivia. Attach what it means for the reader's
  decision in the same breath. If you cannot say why a number matters, question whether it belongs.
- NO VAGUE QUANTITIES OR TIMES. "Recently" hides a date. "Many companies" hides a share. If a card holds
  the specific, the vague word is banned. If no card holds it, drop the claim rather than gesture at it.
- ONE IDEA PER SENTENCE. A sentence carries a subject, a verb and one point. When you catch yourself
  joining two points with "which", "while", "and thereby" or a second comma-clause, split it in two.
- SAY IT AS A VERB, NOT A NOUN. "Evaluate candidates", not "conduct an evaluation of candidates".
  "Decide", not "make a determination". Turning a verb into a noun doubles the words and drains the
  action out of the sentence.
- ACTIVE VOICE. "The panel scored every answer", not "every answer was scored by the panel".
- CUT THE QUALIFIER STACK. One qualifier per claim. "A structured, behaviourally anchored, weighted
  rubric" is three stacked in front of one noun. Keep the one that carries the meaning. The reader
  cannot hold three at once, so all three land as none.
- VARY THE SHAPE. Two long sentences earn a short one. Never let three in a row share the same
  construction. Uniform rhythm reads machine-made even when every fact is right.
- CLAIM ONLY WHAT YOU CAN BACK. No guaranteed outcomes. No "this fixes hiring". Acknowledge the
  trade-off or the limit. Honest beats impressive.

WRITE THE PLAIN WORD. Use, not utilise. Help, not facilitate. Start, not commence. Corporate vocabulary
reads as filler even when the thinking behind it is sharp.

NEVER USE THESE WORDS: streamline, seamless, seamlessly, leverage, robust, empower, elevate, enhance,
crucial, essential, comprehensive, holistic, delve, realm, unravel, foster, harness, cornerstone,
backbone, dynamic, fast-paced, ever-evolving, future-proof, must-have, powerful, kickstart, endeavors,
game-changing, revolutionary, world-class, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, transformative, synergistic,
paradigm, unlock, very, really, actually, significantly, valuable, informed decisions.

NEVER OPEN WITH THESE: "In today's fast-paced landscape", "It's important to note that", "Overall,",
"Additionally,", "Furthermore,", "Remember,", "Let's explore", "Let's be honest", "In short", "But don't
worry", "There you have it".

NEVER USE THESE SHAPES: "it's not just X, it's Y" · "win-win" · "needle in a haystack" · "beacon of
hope" · "on top of their game" · "attract and retain top talent" · "drives results".

MECHANICS:
- No em dashes. Use a comma, a colon, or a full stop. The spaced en dash and the spaced colon are banned
  outright.
- Oxford comma, always. Curly quotes, never straight ones.
- Numbers: spell out one to nine in running prose; numerals for 10 and up. Always numerals for
  percentages (5%), money ($500) and measurements (5 GB). Ranges hyphenated with no spaces (2-3 weeks,
  15-20%). Commas in large numbers (1,000+). Never open a sentence with a numeral: recast it.
- Dates as "July 4, 2026". No ordinals. US spelling throughout.
- Spell out an acronym on first use, then use the short form: "Applicant Tracking System (ATS)".
- Bold sparingly and only for genuine emphasis. Italics for terms. Never underline, never ALL CAPS.
- Gender-neutral, people-first language. Avoid idioms that do not translate.

WHEN A LIST IS RIGHT, AND WHEN IT IS NOT

Most sections should contain NO list at all. A section that is mostly bullets reads as a slide deck, and
a list of near-identical stubs is the most obvious machine-writing tell there is.

Reach for one only when you catch yourself writing "First... Then... Third..." inside a paragraph, and
ALL THREE of these hold:
  · THREE OR MORE items. Two things are a sentence with "and" in it.
  · EACH ITEM STANDS ALONE. If item two only makes sense after item one, it is an argument, not a list,
    and argument belongs in prose.
  · EACH ITEM IS SHORT, a line or two. An item needing four sentences is a paragraph in disguise, and a
    list of paragraphs is harder to read than the prose you started with.

If you write one: NUMBERED when the order matters (steps performed in sequence), DASHED when the items
are parallel and could be read in any order. Bold lead-in on each item. Every item opens the same way,
usually with a verb. Keep the [c...] tags on whichever items carry a fact.

Never reach for a list to break up a long paragraph. That is what a sub-heading is for.

## What we believe

### 1. Skills beat resumes

Judge candidates on demonstrated skills, never on resumes or gut feel. Name the enemy outright ("resume manipulation", "relying on their gut feeling") and offer evidence as the cure.

**How it sounds:** a confident recruiter-to-recruiter argument. State the problem with resumes plainly, then show the skills-first alternative in the same breath, like a colleague who has already made this switch and wants you to catch up.

**Avoid:** praising resumes or credentials as proxies for ability; "gut feel" framed as a valid method; vague talk of "potential" with no assessment behind it.

**Check:** if a claim about a candidate can't be tested, don't write it.

### 2. Fair by design, careful by law

Assessments **reduce bias** and create **a level playing field**, and the process is **defensible under EEOC Uniform Guidelines**. We never claim to eliminate bias. This one outranks every instinct to write a punchier line.

**How it sounds:** closer to a compliance briefing than a slogan when the stakes are legal ("Lawful DEI programs produce diverse hires as an outcome of better processes; they do not set demographic hiring quotas"); warm when the stakes are human ("creating a level playing field and ensuring a fair assessment process").

**Avoid:** "bias-free", "eliminates bias", "removes bias", any absolute claim about the product's fairness, quota-flavored language.

**Test:** could a lawyer sign off on the sentence?

## Naming Testlify

Position Testlify only where it genuinely wins. On a broad topic (recruiting, hiring, talent strategy), write the broad context honestly, then locate Testlify at the assessment and screening slice. Claiming one slice well beats claiming the whole stack vaguely.

|---|---|
| A pre-hire talent-assessment platform: skills tests, role-based assessments, cognitive ability, personality, coding and technical assessments, a large test library, anti-cheat and proctoring, and the candidate experience around screening. | An ATS, a sourcing/CRM tool, an HRIS or payroll system, background checks, onboarding, or a video-interview suite. We integrate with parts of the stack; we don't replace it. |

Say plainly where Testlify isn't the answer, for example very small teams or a niche need. Don't pretend to fit everyone.

**Writing the name:** Testlify is always one word, capital T only, never all-caps and never hyphenated. The possessive is fine ("Testlify's pre-employment assessments"). Generic features and categories stay lowercase ("skills assessment platform", "pre-employment assessments", "hiring plan"). # Voices from the field, The Real Cost of Recruitment in 2026: The Numbers Behind the 2022 Figure Everyone Still Quotes

> This is what people who do this work say in public, gathered from open discussions. It is never a fact, it never carries a source tag, and it never quotes or names a person.

**Coverage.** 9 discussion(s) read and 82 comments, from 5 on linkedin, 4 on reddit. 167 results were looked at across 2 search round(s).

## Agency fee bands are treated as fixed, not negotiable

**What they say.** People on both sides of the table say the percentage is set before the work starts and does not move afterwards. Recruiters describe refusing a discount once a candidate has been accepted, and the buyers who ask for one are described as having failed to set expectations internally. The one softening people do accept is trading a lower rate for something in return: exclusivity, a block of roles, a shorter rebate window, or faster payment terms.

**Strength.** Strong. The same point appears in three separate discussions, from agency recruiters, in-house recruiters and buyers, so it is not one side defending its own margin.

**Serves.** Section 7 (agency fees), and the angle's claim that a cost figure has to be defensible.

**What this changes.** Present the fee bands as a price you either accept or trade against, not one you talk down. Where the section discusses reducing agency cost, the realistic levers are volume, exclusivity and rebate terms, not a lower percentage on a placed hire.

## Recruitment budgets are argued in separate, unequal pots

**What they say.** Several people describe a pattern where a large search fee clears without much friction while a smaller argument runs for weeks over the salary of the same hire. The explanation offered is that the two sit in different budget lines with different scrutiny, and one is easier to treat as a one-off professional fee than the other. Fee spend and payroll spend are simply not challenged with the same intensity.

**Strength.** Moderate. It appears in two discussions with independent agreement from finance-side commenters as well as recruiters, though one of the two is a LinkedIn post where the author is making a public argument.

**Serves.** The angle. This is the boardroom the reader is defending a number in.

**What this changes.** Frame the hard-vs-soft split as also a political one. A figure that survives challenge has to name which pot each cost sits in, because the reader's audience does not scrutinise all of them equally.

## Application volume is up, and screening cost is the reason

**What they say.** Employers describe an inbox they cannot read through, with a routine posting pulling hundreds of applications, many of them generated. The response people report is either more screening work per hire or a retreat to methods that cost calendar time, like in-person rounds and referral-first shortlists. Job seekers in the same threads describe sending far more applications than they used to for the same number of interviews, which is the other half of the same loop.

**Strength.** Strong. It runs through three separate discussions, and the volume claim is made by employers and candidates independently rather than only by the side complaining.

**Serves.** Section 8 (interview load and screening cost).

**What this changes.** The volume rise is not an abstract statistic. Tie it to the section's screening lever directly: the unbilled cost lands on whoever reads the pile, and the choice practitioners actually make is between reading more or paying calendar time in interviews.

## Agencies are used for niche and urgent roles, not general ones

**What they say.** In-house people describe going to agencies only for roles they cannot fill themselves, usually niche or urgent, and handling the rest internally because they get plenty of applicants. Agency-side people describe the same shift from the other end: mid-level headhunting drying up while executive search holds. Both attribute it to a market where a cheap posting produces more candidates than a team can process.

**Strength.** Moderate. Two discussions, but with agreement from both in-house and agency commenters describing the same change from opposite sides.

**Serves.** Section 11 (build or buy) and section 10 (role bands).

**What this changes.** The build-or-buy break-even is not only about volume. Add the second axis practitioners actually use: agencies get the scarce and the urgent roles, which is also why agency-heavy spend concentrates at the top of the role band.

## What did not show up

- **No one quotes a cost-per-hire benchmark.** Across every discussion, not one person cited $4,129, ~$4,700, or any published average. The stale-citation problem is real in published content, but practitioners here argue in fee percentages and dollar amounts on specific hires, not in benchmarks.
- **No one describes calculating cost per hire.** No formula, no denominator, no mention of the ANSI standard or what counts as an internal cost. The metric is discussed as a thing that exists and travels well, never as something someone builds.
- **Nothing on vacancy cost or ramp time as dollars.** Lost productivity and delayed initiatives are named as real costs, but always in general terms. Nobody priced an empty seat per day or costed a ramp period.
- **Nothing on cost per hire segmented by volume.** People discuss whether to use an agency, but no one reports their own cost per hire at a given annual hiring volume, and no break-even hire count appeared anywhere.

# Voices from the field, Running a Hiring Hackathon That Screens for Real Skill, With a Judging Rubric and an AI-Use Policy

> This is a read of what practitioners and candidates say in public discussion threads. None of it is a fact, none of it carries a source tag, and none of it may quote or name a person in the article.

**Coverage.** 12 discussion(s) read and 139 comments, from 1 on blind, 2 on linkedin, 9 on reddit. 145 results were looked at across 2 search round(s).

## Candidates read unpaid build tasks as free labour
**What they say.** Across several threads, people describe multi-hour or multi-day build exercises as work the company extracts without paying, and some report their submitted work later showing up in the company's actual product or marketing. The strongest objection is not the difficulty, it is the imbalance: the exercise costs the candidate hours and costs the employer nothing. A recurring demand is either payment for anything beyond a short task, or a hard cap on how many rounds a candidate is asked to sit.
**Strength.** Strong. Four separate discussions, including one dominated by working designers and one by senior engineers, with high agreement in each. Two of those are venting communities, so the anger is amplified, but the specific complaint about scope and ownership repeats outside them.
**Serves.** Section 1, and the angle. The article positions the hackathon as a work sample test worth its setup cost, so it has to name the cost the candidate carries.
**What this changes.** Section 1 should state the time budget and the ownership rule as part of the format definition, not leave them unsaid. Say who owns the submitted work and that it will not be used in production.

## Ambitious take-home briefs get quietly scored down
**What they say.** One widely-read thread describes a take-home that stacked several distinct tasks into one exercise, and the response from experienced engineers was that the brief tested endurance rather than skill, so a rational candidate either skips it or does the minimum. In a separate thread on live exercises, people describe the opposite working well: one small, deliberately flawed artifact and a discussion about it. The consistent read is that scope creep in the brief destroys the comparison between candidates, because each one guesses differently about what actually counts.
**Strength.** Moderate. Three discussions, spread across two platforms, and the agreement is about the diagnosis rather than the fix.
**Serves.** Section 2 and section 3. What the score must settle drives what the brief may ask for.
**What this changes.** Section 2 should add that the brief has to name which criteria are being scored, so candidates do not spend their budget on the parts the rubric ignores.

## Practitioners split on whether rubrics beat judgment
**What they say.** In the professional discussions, one side argues that "we'll know it when we see it" and personality-based reads are just an excuse to skip the work of defining competencies, and that a structured rubric plus a mixed panel is what surfaces value a single reviewer would miss. The other side argues that any rubric becomes a target candidates optimise for, and that forcing a subjective decision into an objective structure loses the thing you were actually judging. A middle position appears more than once: score the evidence first and lock it, then discuss fit, so rapport cannot retroactively change how the same answer is valued.
**Strength.** Strong. The split runs across three discussions, two on a professional network where people are stating public positions rather than venting, and one among senior engineers.
**Serves.** The angle, and section 4. Published research does not show this split; it presents rubrics as settled good practice.
**What this changes.** Section 3 or 4 should answer the gaming objection directly instead of ignoring it, and the lock-scores-before-discussion sequence is worth naming as an explicit rule.

## Candidates see AI bans as one-sided and ignore them
**What they say.** The most repeated position across the AI threads is that employers screen with AI while telling candidates not to use it, and that this asymmetry makes the ban feel like a status rule rather than a fairness rule. Several people say plainly that they note the instruction and disregard it, and others argue the ban is unenforceable anyway because competent use is undetectable. A quieter but recurring counter-position is that the interesting question is not whether AI was used but whether the candidate can critique what it produced, rather than accepting it whole.
**Strength.** Strong. Five separate discussions across two platforms, with the same complaint appearing in recruiter-side communities as well as candidate-side ones.
**Serves.** Section 7 and section 8. Section 7 rules out the blanket ban, and this is the evidence for why.
**What this changes.** Section 7 should say the ban fails for a specific reason: candidates read it as hypocrisy and comply selectively, which means the ban produces an uneven field rather than a level one. Section 9 should state the employer's own AI use in the same policy, since candidates are already weighing it.

## Confidence in spotting AI use is widely disputed
**What they say.** Interviewers in these threads report they can tell when someone is using AI, usually from stilted phrasing or answers that do not follow the question. Candidates and other practitioners push back hard on that claim, arguing that poor use is visible and competent use is not, so detection selects for clumsiness rather than for honesty. What both sides accept is that a follow-up conversation about specifics, the trade-offs, the problems hit, what was rejected, exposes the gap that reading the artifact does not.
**Strength.** Moderate. Four discussions, with genuine disagreement on detection and genuine agreement on the follow-up conversation.
**Serves.** Section 11. It names the live walkthrough as the step where an undisclosed submission comes apart.
**What this changes.** Section 11 should not promise that judges can detect undisclosed AI from the submission itself. Put the whole weight on the walkthrough and on specific process questions.

- **Nothing on inter-rater reliability, calibration sessions, or judge disagreement.** No one described double-scoring, comparing judges, or measuring agreement. Section 5 is unsupported by field discussion in either direction.
