How the Write Phase turns a pile of research into a finished article

Research finds the material. The write phase decides what makes the cut, checks that every number is real, designs the article, writes it, cleans it, and links it. Three programs, seventeen steps, thirty-three AI prompts. This page walks through every one of them, names the file that does the work, links the exact prompt text, and shows you how to judge the output yourself.

01The one idea

Before any file names, here is the whole thing in four moves. A research bundle comes in at one end. A publish-ready article comes out the other.

📋

1. Vet the material

Research hands over hundreds of fact cards under a menu of candidate headings. The Planner judges each sub-topic, drops the ones that serve nothing, and checks that every number has a real source page behind it.

📐

2. Design the article

The Architect throws away the research outline and designs the real one: which sections, in what order, what each one must deliver, how many words it gets, and what its heading says.

✍

3. Write it

Every section is written at the same time by a separate AI call that can only see its own facts. An editor then stitches them into one continuous piece and writes the intro, takeaways, FAQ and close.

🧹

4. Clean and link it

An AI strips the tells that make writing sound machine-made. Code scrubs invisible characters. Internal and external links go in. The finished draft comes out with every statistic traceable to its source.

The one rule that governs everything

Code counts. The AI judges. If you can count it, a script does it. If two sensible people could disagree about it, the AI does it.

  • Code does: word counts, keyword counts, "did every promised statistic actually land in the draft", "does this link return a real page", "is this source tag one the writer was actually given".
  • The AI does: whether a sub-topic earns its place, whether a page really supports a claim, what a section's heading should say, whether a sentence reads like a human wrote it.

The reason: if you ask an AI "is this title under 60 characters" it eyeballs it and often gets it wrong. len(title) does not guess. And if you ask a script "is this section any good", it invents an arbitrary formula.

The second rule

Nothing is invented. Every fact in the finished article traces back to an evidence card that carries a verbatim quote and a source URL. The writers are handed a fixed list of facts and told they may use nothing else. Every step after the writers, the editor, the wrapper writer, the style cleaner, sees only prose and never the cards, so none of them can add a fact. Code then checks their output and strips any source tag that was not there before.

02The words you need first

Every term used on this page, defined once, in plain English. If a later section uses a word you do not recognise, it is here.

Bundle — what the research phase hands over for one topic. Two files: a structure JSON holding the candidate headings and all the evidence, and a cover sheet pointing at everything else (the search-results study, the brand voice files, the research brief).
Card (evidence card) — one fact, with proof attached. It carries an id number, a one-line summary ("gloss"), the source text word for word ("verbatim"), and the URL of the page it came from. A single article can arrive with over 3,000 of them. Cards are the only facts a writer is allowed to use.
[c412] tag — the marker a writer puts straight after a fact, naming the card it came from. It travels with the sentence through every later step, which is how the finished article can turn each statistic into a source link, and how code can catch a fact that was invented.
H1 / H2 / H3 — heading levels. H1 is the article's title. H2s are its sections. H3s are the sub-topics inside a section. Research delivers a menu of candidate H2s, each holding several H3s, and the cards usually hang off the H3s.
Box — when the Architect redesigns the article, every surviving H3 is turned into a numbered box (#1, #2, #3…) with its cards inside. The AI designing the structure answers only with headlines and box numbers, so it can shuffle the material freely but cannot invent material that does not exist.
Spine — one short paragraph saying what the whole article argues, who it is for, and what the reader can do at the end. The Architect writes it before anything else, and every later step is judged against it. If a section does not serve the spine, it does not belong.
The world (about / not-about) — two lines, decided back in the research phase, stating what this article is about and what it is deliberately not about. It exists because the same words mean different things in different fields. An article about Type D personality in hiring must not pull in cardiology research, even though every search result mentions it. The world lines ride into almost every prompt in the pipeline as a first test: wrong world is a harder no than merely off-topic.
Archetype — which of eight article shapes this piece is: a definitional explainer, a how-to guide, a listicle, a comparison, a glossary, a data report, a template resource, or plain editorial ("common spine"). The archetype decides which structure prompt runs and which shape rules apply.
Gap / table stake / PAA / related search — the four kinds of promise a sub-topic can serve. A gap is something no competitor covers, so it is ours to own. A table stake is a topic every competitor covers, so leaving it out looks thin. PAA is "People Also Ask", the questions Google shows under a search result. A related search is what Google lists at the bottom of the page. Each one gets an id (G1, T2, Q3, R4) so a sub-topic can point at exactly which promise it delivers.
Orphan — a good sub-topic whose parent section was cut. Rather than lose it, the Planner asks an AI to file it into the best surviving section. Nothing valuable is dropped just because its neighbour was weak.
Benched — material the Architect chose not to use. It is not deleted. It is listed with a one-line reason, so you can see what was left on the table and disagree if you want.
Word band — the target length range for the article, worked out in the research phase from how long the currently-winning pages are. A band like 3,000 to 4,000 words. The Architect splits the midpoint of that band across the sections.
Volume and KD — two numbers from search data. Volume is roughly how many people search that phrase per month. KD is keyword difficulty, a 0 to 100 score for how hard it would be to rank. A section keyword has to clear 100 monthly searches and stay under 40 difficulty to be considered.
Slop — the patterns that make text read as machine-written. Em dashes everywhere, words like "delve", "robust", "landscape", "seamless", three-item lists on a loop, every sentence the same length, "it's not X, it's Y". The slop step removes them without touching a single fact.
Provenance — the record of which card each sentence's facts came from. Built by code from the [c] tags, not reported by the AI about itself.
Eval page — a self-contained HTML page the pipeline builds for you after each stage, showing what it decided and why. Seven of them per article. Section 09 covers what is on each.
Atomic save — every file is written to a temporary name first and then renamed into place. A crash halfway through leaves the old complete file, never a half-written one. This matters because every step skips itself if its output file already exists, so a half-written file would be silently trusted.

05The Planner, in detail

The Planner never writes a word. Its whole job is to decide what is true and what is worth covering, so that everything downstream is working with clean material. All numbers below are from the five real articles already run through the engine.

What arrives from research

The bundle is a menu, not an article. Its own instructions say "the writer selects sections and does the wording." A typical bundle carries around 18 candidate sections, each with several sub-topics, and the evidence usually hangs off the sub-topics rather than the sections. Here is what one evidence card looks like.

card_id
The number the article cites it by. Research cards keep their original ids. Cards the Architect researches later start at 9001, so you can always tell where a fact came from.
gloss
A one-line summary of the fact. This is what names the subject, which is why the source judge reads it: the same number about a different company is not support.
verbatim
The source text word for word. Nothing is paraphrased into a card.
source_urls
Where it came from. This is the field the source-verification step checks, repairs, or cuts the card over.
tag
ownpage or storm, telling you whether it came from reading a competitor's page or from the research phase's own investigation.

Step 2: the 45% rule, and why arithmetic decides it

The tagging is a judgment, so an AI does it. The survival decision is a count, so code does it. That split is the whole design.

1 AI tags every sub-topic› 2 code counts the tagged share› 3 45% or more, the section lives› 4 AI rehomes the orphans

The tagger gets one call per candidate section, and it must point at the card that proves each tag. The prompt is blunt about the failure it is preventing:

"EVERY TAG MUST NAME ITS PROOF. For each tag, give the card id(s) from THIS H3 whose text actually contains the thing the tag claims. If you cannot point to a card, you do not have that tag. Being on the same broad topic as a promise is not serving it."

Before it tags anything it runs the world test, which was added after a measured failure: 152 of 478 sub-topics across one article came back untagged, and 71 of the survivors held on by the loosest tag alone. The fix was to show the tagger each card's source, not just its words.

"Where a subject shares a WORD with another world, the card's SOURCE is often the only thing that reveals it: a university's page about a student prize contest, a vendor selling a different product, a clinical or academic use of the same term. Read the source, not only the words."

Step 3: the source police

This is the step that separates the engine from a content mill. Every number that a reader would expect a citation for is checked against the page it credits, and the check is a real download and a real read.

1 code finds cards with numbers› 2 AI picks which need proof› 3 fetch the claimed page› 4 AI judge reads it› 5 hunt for a better source› 6 fix the link, or cut the card

Five design details worth knowing

  • The judge reads the right part of the page. Taking the first few thousand characters showed it the site's navigation menu and falsely cut around 21% of claims. It now reads the page's opening plus the passages around the claim's numbers.
  • Same number, different subject, is not support. The judge is told: a page stating the same figure about a different company, or about the market in general, gets a no.
  • One request at a time per website. Bursting a host makes it serve a block page instead of content, which reads as a failed verification when the source was fine.
  • A bad URL is bad for everyone. Any URL proven wrong is remembered, and at the end every card citing it is rechecked even if it was never picked as worth checking.
  • Mercy rules. A page that will not load, because of a paywall or a block, is kept and flagged rather than punished. A search that failed because the service was down keeps the card and flags it. A card is only ever deleted after a judge said no and a hunt found nothing.
The honesty check built into this step

A run where the search service died would report zero cards cut and zero bad URLs, which looks identical to a perfectly clean article. Step 4: what freeze will and will not stop

🛑

Hard flags

The plan is not frozen. Fix and rerun.
  • No sections at all
  • A section with zero sub-topics
  • A sub-topic with zero evidence
  • Missing headline, primary keyword or archetype
  • Word band missing or zero
⚠

Soft notes

Recorded, never blocking. You decide.
  • A promise nothing in the article serves, listed as a "HOLE"
  • Fewer than three sections
  • More than 15 cards cut by source verification
  • Cards kept with sources that would not load

Why section keywords are gated before they are bought

Most sections should not target a search phrase. An article has one main search target, its H1, and a handful of sections that answer a real search of their own. The rest exist to carry the argument. Giving those a keyword produces a forced heading and wastes a paid lookup.

"Expect roughly a third to a half of the sections to be YES. If you are saying yes to nearly all of them, you are being too generous, go back and cut to the ones a stranger would genuinely search for."

Three details in the hunt that came from real failures:

  • Seeds are read from the evidence, not the heading. "A section whose heading says pricing but whose evidence is all commission percentages is a section about commission rates. Seed it that way."
  • Seeds must be 2 or 3 words. The keyword index matches on the phrase itself, so a five-word seed returns nothing and the section ends up with no keyword at all. The prompt gives the example: write "hackathon ai policy", not "AI policy for a hiring hackathon".
  • Returning nothing is an explicitly good answer. Both the gate and the pick say so in capitals. Fit beats volume, because a bigger number for a phrase the section does not answer means the page ranks and the reader bounces.

This is the rule that lets the article carry worked examples without inventing evidence.

Two more fact rules that shape how the article reads:

The editor is the one step that sees the whole article as prose. Everything it may do is enumerated, and it must report every edit it makes.

1 add a transition 2 cut a repetition 3 shorten a second definition 4 fix a broken back-reference 5 weave in a keyword

The keyword move is the interesting one, because it is where search phrases enter an article whose writers never saw them:

"Find sentences that ALREADY express the idea. 'The time it takes to fill a role' becomes 'time to fill'. 'What a hire costs you' becomes 'cost per hire'. Same meaning, exact phrase. Keywords that find no honest home are reported as skipped, with a reason, rather than forced in. The editor may not touch a heading, may not reorder sections, may not add a fact, and may not touch a source tag. Code audits the tags afterwards either way.

Wrapper: the intro rule and the FAQ test

The intro is the only place in the pipeline where specific sentence shapes are banned by name, because it is the one place an AI reliably reaches for them.

Wrong: "This article breaks down hard versus soft costs by role and industry."

write-body.mdwrite-body · 111 lines

The prompt that actually writes the article. One call per section, all running at once.

  • Split into FACT RULES and WRITING RULES, because the two failure modes are different: one produces a lie, the other produces boring prose.
  • Draws the fact-versus-craft line explicitly, so a section can carry a worked example without inventing evidence, as long as the example contains zero invented specifics.
  • It is told the other sections are being written in parallel and it can see only their briefs: "Never write 'as we saw above' or quote prose that does not exist yet."
  • Names the persona and immediately forbids addressing them: "think about them, but NEVER name or address them."
  • The length line refuses to become a quota: "That is a target, not a quota. If the facts do not justify it, write less. NEVER pad to reach it."
  • Warns the writer about the one thing nothing downstream will catch: "The editor who reads the finished article only checks for repetition BETWEEN sections; repetition inside your own paragraphs is yours to prevent and nobody else will catch it."

blend.mdblend · 65 lines

The editor's pass. Makes separately written sections read as one continuous piece, and weaves the search phrases into prose that already says those things.

  • Exactly five permitted moves, listed and numbered, and everything else is in a "WHAT YOU MUST NOT DO" block.
  • The keyword move is defined as a word swap inside an existing sentence: never a new sentence, never a new fact, never a repeat to raise a count.
  • "Do NOT rewrite prose that reads fine. This is a seam repair, not a rewrite. Most sentences must come through untouched."
  • It must report every edit and every keyword it skipped, with a reason, which is what makes the edit trail on the review page possible.

wrapper.mdwrapper · 130 lines

The longest prompt after the ruleset. Writes the intro, the key takeaways, the five FAQs, the close, and the touch-ups to the two sections the new seams affect.

  • Opens with the constraint that makes everything else safe: "you have no sources. Every fact, number, name and claim you use must ALREADY be in the article above."
  • Bans ten specific opening shapes by name and shows a wrong-versus-right example, because this is the single place an AI most reliably writes about the page instead of informing the reader.
  • The five-part FAQ test carefully separates the question from the facts: (a) is about the question, (b) is about the facts you answer it with. It says so in the prompt, because they get conflated.
  • The close is grounded in the company's real feature list and told never to imply the product solves something the article showed to be unsolvable.
  • Ends with a note on brand mentions: "Never insert it to hit a quota, a forced mention costs more trust than it buys."

slop-rules.mdthe shared ruleset · 402 lines

Not a prompt, but the rulebook that gets loaded into every slop call. The full avoid-ai-writing ruleset carried over word for word, plus this pipeline's own overriding rules.

  • Opens with five rules that override everything below them on conflict: facts untouchable, source tags stay attached, headings never changed, the voice stays, and an em-dash budget of zero.
  • Sorts vocabulary into three tiers. Tier 1 words appear 5 to 20 times more often in AI text and are replaced on sight. Tier 2 only counts when two appear in the same paragraph. Tier 3 only counts at roughly 3% density. That tiering is what stops false positives on ordinary words.
  • Around 45 named patterns beyond vocabulary, including the rule of three, synonym cycling, false ranges, rhetorical question openers, emotional flatline, and unfilled placeholders.
  • Everything carries a severity: P0 credibility killers, P1 obvious AI smell, P2 polish. The prompt only forces P0 and P1.
  • This is the one place to tune a style rule. Editing it changes every article from the next run on.

inline-links.mdlinks · 40 lines

Places 3 to 5 internal links by laying anchors over words already in the text.

  • Value first: "a reader who clicks it must land on a page that gives a genuinely deeper or more practical treatment of the exact thing those words talk about. The link is a promise. When in doubt, do not link."
  • The anchor must be quoted character for character from the body, never from a heading, and the prompt warns that a mismatch is thrown away by code and the link is lost.
  • Spread rules stop clustering: at most two per paragraph, at most one per section unless two genuinely earn it, and no two links may use the same wording.
  • Asks for at least one product page where it fits, and explicitly permits skipping it: "never force the product."

read-more.mdlinks · 46 lines

Decides whether the article earns a single added line pointing to a page that continues what it started. Usually the answer is no.

  • "ZERO is the normal, expected outcome." One is common when it happens, two is the absolute maximum.
  • Candidates arrive with their real opening text, not just their titles, so "a thin stub does not qualify however good its title sounds".
  • It may lightly smooth the sentences on either side, but must report each adjustment word for word, because code applies and audits them and an unreported change is thrown away.
  • Carries the house style rules itself, because this line is added after the style pass: no em dashes, no "dive into", no "check out", no exclamation mark. "It ships exactly as you write it."

external-links.mdlinks · 38 lines

Chooses which of the article's many verified citations become visible links and appear in the Sources list.

  • "Judge them AGAINST EACH OTHER, not one by one, credibility is relative, and you can see the whole field."
  • Primary sources beat write-ups about them: "a page that merely repeats another source's number loses to the source itself."
  • Claims carrying a load-bearing number are marked with a star in the candidate list, and the sources behind those win ties, because that is where a reader wants a link they can check.
  • One per domain, two at the very most, so no single site dominates the Sources list.

09How to check the output yourself

Every run builds seven review pages plus a front door, automatically. They are plain HTML files that open in any browser, and they are built only from files the pipeline already wrote, so any of them can be rebuilt for any past article at any time. Nothing on them decides anything.

Start here

Open index.html in the article's output folder. It lists the five stages in order, each linking to its page, and it tells you plainly why to read them in that order: "Each one shows a decision the machine made, so you can catch a bad call early instead of finding it in the finished article." A page that has not been built yet is marked rather than hidden.

The banner at the top is the thing to read first

The page states what percentage of claims were actually judged and whether the run is trustworthy. This exists because a run where the search service died reports zero cuts and zero bad URLs, which looks exactly like a perfectly clean article. On the five real articles that number ranged from 98% down to 10%. Only one earned the stamp.

The page also answers "did anything collapse?" A card is the smallest unit, so a sub-topic that loses all its evidence dies, and a section that loses all its sub-topics dies with it. When nothing collapsed the page says so in words rather than showing an empty box, so you can tell "clean" apart from "not rendered".

The article exactly as it will publish, with four things layered on top that the published version will not have.

Designed, not yet built

Nothing here runs today. Do not assume these checks are happening.
  • MEASURE. The countable-facts report: finished length against the word band, sentence and paragraph statistics, citation counts per domain, heading hierarchy, the 40 to 60 word liftable-answer check. This is why the length overrun in section 06 is currently unpoliced.
  • REVIEW and EDIT. An AI reviewer fed those facts, sorting findings into critical, high and quick-win, then an editor pass, looping up to three times.
  • The GATE. The single publish checklist that has to pass before anything ships, with anything failing three times going to a human.
  • Meta title and description. Research does not produce them and the write phase does not yet write them.
  • PUBLISH. The WordPress draft, the Bing submission, and stamping the topic queue as written.
  • Author box and dates. Publish-time work, and the author identity is still an open decision.
Three known weaknesses, stated plainly
  • Drafts run long. All five came out 25% to 45% above their word band. Nothing measures this yet, so nothing pushes back.
  • Source verification often does not finish. Four of the five runs judged under two thirds of their claims, one as low as 10%, usually from hitting the 250-search cap or running out of search credit. The pages say so rather than hiding it, but those claims ship on unverified links.
  • The style pass leaves work behind on long articles. On the two longest drafts it removed 80% and 54% of em dashes rather than all of them. The mechanical scrub afterwards catches dashes it can handle deterministically, but the judgment-based rewrites are incomplete.
On which AI model writes the articles

The default chain is DeepSeek first, then Claude, then Codex. A blind A/B across the five articles, with independent reviewers and a judge, found Claude better on prose and discipline and DeepSeek better on structural compliance, and found that most of the differences were prompt gaps rather than model gaps. Those prompt gaps have since been fixed and the default was deliberately left unchanged. One gap remains open: nothing currently records which model produced which output.

What research decides, and write does not

What the article should say. The candidate structure. Which sources exist. The primary keyword, chosen from real search data with a world check. The target length band. The reader persona. The write phase inherits all of it and is deliberately not allowed to overrule the primary keyword.

What write decides, and research does not

Which of the candidate sections survive. The exact wording. Which citations are chosen and which are visible. The internal links. The section keywords, researched against the sections as actually built. Every heading. The title. And the proof that the finished draft matches the plan.

One thing the write phase deliberately refuses to do

It does not decide whether an idea is worth writing. That gate lives upstream, in the asset engine, which classifies every idea as write it, build it, collect data first, or route it elsewhere. By the time a bundle reaches the write phase, that decision has already been made and the phase simply trusts it.
