02The words you need

Eight terms used throughout. Everything else on this page is plain English.

TermWhat it means
CardOne fact with proof attached: a summary, the source text word for word, and a link. The only facts a writer is allowed to use. One article can arrive with 3,000 of them.
SpineOne paragraph saying what the whole article argues, for whom, and what the reader can do at the end. Written first. Everything is judged against it.
The worldTwo lines set in research: what this article is about, and what it is deliberately not about. It stops a Type D personality article pulling in cardiology research just because every search result mentions it.
GapSomething no competitor covers. Ours to own.
Table stakeSomething every competitor covers. Leaving it out looks thin.
OrphanA good sub-topic whose parent section got cut. Refiled rather than lost.
BenchedMaterial the design step chose not to use. Listed with a reason, never deleted silently.
SlopThe patterns that make text read machine-written. Em dashes everywhere, "delve", "robust", "landscape", three-item lists on a loop, every sentence the same length.

03The five rules everything obeys

These are the promises the machine makes. If you want to argue with the output, argue with these first.

🔒

1. Nothing is invented

Every fact traces to a card with a quote and a link. Writers get a fixed list of facts and may use nothing else. Every step after them sees only the prose, never the cards, so none of them can add a fact.

🔍

2. Every number is checked

The page a claim credits is downloaded and read. Same number about a different company does not count. A wrong link gets repaired. If nothing supports it, the fact is cut.

🌎

3. One world only

Material from a neighbouring field is dropped even when it matches perfectly. The same words mean different things to different audiences, and a wrong-world fact reads plausible and serves nobody.

🎯

4. Fit beats volume

A keyword is only used where it names what the section actually delivers. A big search number for a phrase the section does not answer means the page ranks and the reader bounces.

👁

5. Nothing is dropped silently

Every cut section, benched item, skipped keyword and rejected link is written down with a reason, and shown to you on a review page.

✅

And one honesty rule

When a check does not finish, the machine says so instead of reporting a clean result. A dead search service would otherwise look identical to a perfect article.

04Stage 1: Plan

Decides what the article will cover, and whether it is true. Writes nothing.

1

Route the format

  • Eight shapes. Explainer, how-to, listicle, comparison, glossary, data report, template, editorial.
  • Judged, not looked up. The AI weighs the queue label, the title, the angle and the pages currently winning on Google.
  • Why it matters. The shape decides how the article gets built. Wrong shape, wrong article.
2

Tag every sub-topic

  • The question. What does this sub-topic contribute? The angle, a gap, a table stake, a question people ask, or a related search.
  • Proof required. Every tag must name the card that proves it. No card, no tag.
  • World test first. Wrong-world material gets no tag whatever it matches.
  • Calibrated. Most sub-topics honestly earn 1 to 3 tags. More than four means it is matching words, not judging.
3

Cut by arithmetic

  • The bar. A section survives if 45% of its sub-topics earned a tag, and keeps only the tagged ones.
  • Below the bar. The section dies. Its good sub-topics become orphans.
  • Orphans refiled. An AI puts each one in the best surviving section. Nothing valuable is lost to a weak neighbour.
  • Why arithmetic. The tagging is a judgment, so an AI does it. The survival cut is a count, so code does it.
4

Check every number

  • Pick what matters. An AI decides which numbers a reader would expect a citation for. "Step 3" and "in 2026" are skipped.
  • Read the real page. The claimed source is downloaded and judged. Same number about a different company is not support.
  • Repair before cutting. A wrong link triggers a web hunt for the real source. Only if that fails is the fact cut.
  • One bad page poisons all. Any URL proven wrong is remembered, and every other claim citing it is rechecked.
  • Mercy. A paywalled page is kept and flagged, not punished. A failed search never deletes a fact.
5

Freeze the plan

  • Stops the run: no sections, a section with no evidence, a missing title or keyword or word band.
  • Flags but continues: a promise nothing covers, fewer than three sections, a lot of cards cut.
  • No new judgments. It only checks shape. Every decision already happened.

05Stage 2: Design

Throws away the research outline and designs the real article. This is the step that decides what the reader experiences.

1

Design the structure

  • Material gets numbered. Every surviving sub-topic becomes a numbered box with its facts inside.
  • The AI answers in box numbers. So it can merge, split, reorder and rename anything, and cannot invent material that does not exist.
  • Order is enforced. Spine first, then each section's job, then its heading, then its boxes. A heading written before the job exists is a guess you justify backwards.
  • No padding. If a section cannot be built from material that genuinely fits, it is left empty or it raises a research request.
  • Last chance on the world. After this step a box becomes a section and gets written.
2

Fill the gaps

  • Only if asked. Runs when the design step flagged a section as short of material.
  • Per gap. Plan 3 searches, download up to 15 pages that load, build new cards from what they say.
  • One page per card. Blending two sources into one card is banned, because it cannot be traced back.
3

Budget the words

  • By importance, not volume. A section with few facts can still be the heart of the piece.
  • Explicitly banned: allocating length by how many cards a section happens to hold.
  • Midpoint, not ceiling. The band's midpoint is distributed, so there is room to trim.
  • Nothing under 4%. Anything that small should not be its own section.
4

Research a keyword per section

  • Gate first, free. Would a person type this section's subject into Google as its own search? Most sections should be no.
  • Expect a third to a half. Saying yes to nearly all of them means being too generous.
  • Seeded from the evidence. A section whose heading says "pricing" but whose facts are all commission percentages is a section about commission rates.
  • Real data only. Candidates must clear 100 monthly searches and stay under 40 difficulty.
  • None is a good answer. Both the gate and the pick say so explicitly.
5

Write every heading

  • Evidence wins. The job was written before research came back. Where they disagree, what the section actually holds wins.
  • Under 60 characters, counted while writing, not after.
  • One keyword, never two. A heading holding two search phrases reads as written for a machine.
  • Specific, never generic. "Overview" and "Key considerations" are named as the AI's own default failure.
  • Title last, written after seeing every finished heading, so it cannot promise what the article does not deliver.

The fairness rule, on comparison articles
  • If we are one of the options, we appear in every table and ranked list the rivals appear in, on the same scale.
  • We may lose. The prompt says to say so when the evidence says so.
  • Why. A grid that scores every rival but not the publisher reads as though the publisher has something to hide.
  • Switched off when the article compares something we do not sell against, so we are never inserted where we do not belong.

06Stage 3: Write

One writer per section, all at once, then five passes that each improve the article in exactly one way.

1

Write the sections

  • Sees its own facts only. So it cannot use a fact it was not given.
  • Sees every other section's brief, but never their text. So twelve writers pull one way and none can say "as we saw above" about prose that does not exist yet.
  • Keyword blind on purpose. Nobody writing prose is thinking about search phrases.
  • Product free by default. That flips only for a section genuinely about the brand.
  • Facts vs craft. A statistic must come from a card. A worked example may be composed, with zero invented specifics.
2

Stitch it together

  • Five permitted moves. Add a transition, cut a repetition, shorten a second definition, fix a broken reference, swap in a keyword.
  • Keywords are word swaps only. Where a sentence already says the thing in vaguer words. Never a new sentence, never bent to fit.
  • Skipped is reported. A keyword with no honest home is listed as skipped, with a reason.
  • Sees no facts. So it cannot add one. Code then strips any source tag that appeared out of nowhere.
3

Write the wrapper

  • Intro opens with the answer, not a description of the article. Ten opening shapes are banned by name.
  • Exactly five FAQs, each passing a five-part test, and no two may share an answer.
  • Cannot pad. Fewer than five questions passing means fewer than five ship, with reasons.
  • The close is grounded in the real feature list, and is a forward call to action, not a recap.
4

Strip the AI tells

  • The AI proposes, code decides. Every number and every source tag must be identical before and after.
  • Any mismatch and that block keeps its original text, logged loudly.
  • Clean text is untouched. A block with no tells comes back character for character.
  • One rulebook. Tuning a style rule is a change in one place that applies to every future article.
5

Lay in the links

  • Internal, 3 to 5. Anchors laid over words already in the text. Nothing added or reworded.
  • Read more, usually zero. A much higher bar: the target must build on what the reader just learned, not merely relate to it.
  • External, up to 10. Chosen from our own verified citations. Primary sources beat write-ups about them. Competitors removed automatically.
  • Integrity check. Undo every declared change and the text must match exactly. Anything undeclared is reverted.
  • Dead links dropped. Every link is checked for a real response before it ships.
6

Scrub and assemble

  • Invisible characters removed. One real draft carried 31 invisible spaces sitting inside "85 %" and "New York City". No human types one.
  • Dashes and spacing fixed by rule, not by judgment, so it never misses one and never rewrites a word.
  • Curly quotes left alone. Word, Docs and macOS all curl quotes, so they are normal in human prose.
  • Keyword coverage counted from the finished text. A count the AI reported about itself would be a guess.

07Every prompt in the engine

Thirty-three prompts, one per AI decision. Each one is a plain text file you can read and change. Click any name to read the whole thing.

Two things worth knowing before you open one
  • The blanks get filled in. Anything in double braces is swapped for real data before the call: the title, the angle, the evidence, the world lines.
  • This is where the thinking lives. If output is wrong, the fix is almost always a prompt, not the code.

Stage 1: Plan · 10 prompts

PromptWhat it doesThe rule that matters most
route-archetype Picks which of the eight article shapes this is. Weighs the format label, the title, the angle and the winning pages together. No single input outranks the others. One care: a "news" piece whose real value is citable numbers becomes a data report.
extract-winners Pulls the gaps, the table stakes and the drift warnings out of the competitor study. Every item lifted word for word. A list that is genuinely absent comes back empty, because an invented gap becomes a fake hole that drags unrelated facts in.
extract-word-band Reads the target length out of the research brief. Returns zeros rather than guessing if the line is missing.
vet-lists Throws out the Google questions that belong to a different field. Broader is fine, a different world is not. For a Type D article, "what are type A, B, C, D personalities" stays, because it still serves this reader.
tag-h3s The big one. Decides what every sub-topic contributes. Untagged means dropped. Every tag must name the card that proves it. And it is told to read each card's source, not just its words, because a university prize page or a clinical use of the same term will otherwise match.
place-orphans Refiles good sub-topics whose section was cut. Nothing is dropped here at all. Even a poor fit has to be placed, and flagged so a human sees it.
verify-worthy Decides which numbers actually need a verified source. Also flags claims with no number at all: anything crediting a named study, or characterising a rival's product, because publishing that unchecked is a public claim about someone else's business.
source-judge Reads a page and answers: does this prove this claim? Yes only if it is about the same subject. The same number about a different company, or about the market in general, is not support.
source-queries Plans the searches that find the real source. The subject matters as much as the number. Searching a bare figure just finds whoever else quoted it.
search-urls The free fallback when the paid search runs out of credit. Real URLs only, never constructed or guessed. Without that line a model happily assembles a plausible-looking address.

Stage 2: Design · 16 prompts

PromptWhat it doesThe rule that matters most
structure-simple Designs the article body. Used for explainers, how-tos, glossaries, data reports and editorial. Never pad a section to fill it out. If it cannot be built from material that genuinely fits, leave it empty. Anything left out that carried a promise needs a written reason.
structure-listicle Same, for listicles, where each item gets its own section. Sets the per-item contract: the labelled parts every single item must end with, taken from what the angle actually promised. A field only half the items could fill "is not a contract, it is a wish."
structure-comparison Same, for "best X", "X vs Y" and "alternatives to X". Carries the fairness rule: if we are one of the options we appear in every table the rivals do, and may score below them.
structure-template Same, for an article whose real value is a download. Also decides what the download is, and which content goes inside it rather than on the page.
detect-items Finds the actual list items a listicle presents. Gets the grain right: individual questions, not question categories. And an item survives only if the material holds evidence about that item specifically.
detect-entities Finds the named options a comparison is choosing between. The test is a shortlist: would a buyer put these against each other? An adjacent-category product is not an option however often it appears.
find-yardsticks Picks the 4 to 6 axes every option gets measured on. Two conditions at once: a buyer genuinely weighs it, and we have material on it. Plus an anti-overlap rule: "four axes that are all about detection is one axis, not four."
filter-tools Drops options we cannot honestly cover, so no section is written on nothing. Makes it show its work, then self-audits: if a dropped option has more evidence than a kept one, it misread the material and has to recount.
plan-queries Plans searches for a section short of material. Put the audience into the query itself, or the results drift into a neighbouring field and every card built from them looks plausible and is wrong.
extract-cards Builds new fact cards from freshly downloaded pages. One card summarises one page. Blending two sources is banned, because it cannot be traced back. And "never force weak cards" if the pages turned up nothing.
allocate-words Decides each section's share of the article. Judge by importance, not by how much evidence a section happens to hold. "A section with few cards can still be the heart of the piece."
keyword-gate Decides which sections deserve a keyword at all, before anything is bought. Expect a third to a half to qualify. "If you are saying yes to nearly all of them, you are being too generous."
section-seeds Writes the short phrases to look up for a section. Read the evidence, not the heading. "A section whose heading says pricing but whose evidence is all commission percentages is a section about commission rates."
pick-section-keyword Chooses one keyword from the real search data, or none. Fit first, volume second. "Returning nothing is a good answer. Do not stretch."
write-heading Writes each section's final published heading. A four-step keyword ladder that is allowed to end in none: "a well-researched keyword that does not fit is still a keyword that does not fit."
write-h1 Writes the title, last, after seeing every heading. It must not promise something the headings do not deliver. If the planned title already works, it is returned unchanged.

Stage 3: Write · 7 prompts and 1 rulebook

PromptWhat it doesThe rule that matters most
write-body Writes one section. This is the prompt that actually produces the article. Split into fact rules and writing rules, because a broken fact and boring prose are different failures. And it warns the writer about the one thing nothing downstream catches: repetition inside its own paragraphs.
blend Makes the separately written sections read as one piece, and weaves in the keywords. Exactly five permitted moves, everything else forbidden. "Most sentences must come through untouched."
wrapper Writes the intro, the takeaways, the 5 FAQs and the close. Bans ten specific opening shapes by name, with an example. Wrong: "This article breaks down hard versus soft costs." Right: "Two thirds of what a hire costs never appears on an invoice."
slop Removes the AI writing tells from one block of text. Tells the model what code will check afterwards: "a violation throws your whole block away." And style always loses to accuracy.
slop-rules rulebook The style rulebook loaded into every slop call. Around 45 named patterns plus a tiered word list. Words are tiered, so common words are only flagged at high density. This is the one place to tune a style rule, and it applies to every future article.
inline-links Places 3 to 5 internal links over words already in the text. The link is a promise: the reader must land on something genuinely deeper on that exact thing. "When in doubt, do not link."
read-more Decides whether the article earns one added line pointing to a deeper page. "Zero is the normal, expected outcome." Candidates are shown with their real opening text, so a thin stub cannot win on a good title.
external-links Picks which of our verified citations become visible links. Judged against each other, not one by one. A page that merely repeats another source's number loses to the source itself.

08How to check the output

Every run builds eight review pages automatically. They open in any browser. Nothing on them decides anything, so they can be rebuilt for any past article.

The four-minute version
  • Sources page. Is the trustworthy banner green, and how many claims were cut or never checked?
  • Blueprint page. Do you agree with the spine, and does every heading match its job?
  • Selection page. Scan the drop list for anything that should have survived.
  • Article page. Does the keyword panel show the keyword in the title and the opening, and do three random statistics each give you a real source?
PageWhat is on itWhat to look for
1. Gather
what we started with
  • The shape it routed to, and the word band
  • Which Google questions were kept and dropped
  • How much evidence sits behind each candidate section
  • The competitor study: our gaps and their table stakes
  • Wrong shape means everything downstream is built wrong.
  • A wrongly dropped question becomes a topic the article never covers.
  • Two cards behind a section is thin before anyone has judged anything.
2. Selection
what made the cut
  • Everything dropped, with the reason, first on the page
  • What was kept, thinnest evidence at the top
  • Close calls, lowest first
  • Orphans and where they went
  • A good topic in the drop list means the tagger misread it.
  • A section resting on one card is worth killing now, not after it is written.
  • A section that survived at 0.46 against a 0.45 bar is a coin flip you can overrule.
3. Sources
do the numbers check out
  • A banner saying what share of claims was actually judged
  • One row per claim, filterable by verdict
  • Repaired rows show the old link, the new one, and the sentence that proves it
  • Read the banner first. Everything else is meaningless if the check did not finish.
  • Unloadable means paywalled and never verified. Worth a manual look.
  • Unchecked means it is shipping on its original, unverified link.
4. Blueprint
the plan for the article
  • The spine, in one paragraph
  • Every keyword decision, including the refusals and why
  • The bench: what was not used, and why
  • Every section with its job, heading and word target
  • The cheapest place to catch a bad article. Nothing is written yet.
  • If you disagree with the spine, stop here. Everything below serves it.
  • A heading that does not match its job means the section will not deliver.
5. Article
the finished piece
  • The article exactly as it will publish
  • Hover any statistic for its claim and source link
  • Hover any highlight to see what kind of keyword it is
  • A keyword panel counting what actually landed
  • An edit trail of what each pass changed
  • Fastest fact check available. You never leave the article to see where a number came from.
  • The panel counts are done by code, not reported by the AI about itself.
6. Slop
what the cleanup changed		 Every change, red for removed, green for what replaced it, plus every block the code guard rejected. Read a few replacements and judge whether they read better. A rejected block still carries its tells.
7. Links
every link decision		 Internal, read-more and external links, plus the rejections with reasons. Click the internal ones and ask whether the target really is deeper. Check the external list is primary sources, not blogs quoting primary sources.
8. Cost
time and spend		 How long each step took and what it cost, using the providers' own figures rather than estimates. Empty for the five pilot articles. The page works but no ledger was recorded for those runs, so it fills in from the next run onward.

Designed, not built

None of this runs. Do not assume these checks happen.
  • Measurement. Finished length against the target, sentence and paragraph stats, heading hierarchy, the 40 to 60 word snippet answers.
  • The review and edit loop. An AI reviewer sorting findings into critical, high and quick-win, then an editing pass.
  • The publish gate. The single checklist that has to pass before anything ships.
  • Meta title and description. Nobody writes them yet.
  • Publishing. The WordPress draft and the search-engine submission.
  • Author box and dates. The author identity is still an open decision.
Three weaknesses, stated plainly
  • Drafts run long. All five came out 25% to 45% over their target length. Nothing measures this yet, so nothing pushes back.
  • Source checking often does not finish. Four of five runs judged under two thirds of their claims, one as low as 10%. The pages say so, but those claims ship on unverified links.
  • The style pass tires on long articles. On the two longest drafts it removed 80% and 54% of the em dashes, not all of them.
Where this sits
  • Research decides what to say, the candidate structure, the sources, the main keyword and the target length.
  • This phase decides what survives, the exact wording, the citations, the links, every heading and the title.
  • It never re-researches. It assembles and verifies.
  • It does not decide whether an idea is worth writing. That gate lives further upstream.
